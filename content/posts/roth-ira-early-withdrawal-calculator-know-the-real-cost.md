## Why a Roth IRA is different when you withdraw early

A Roth IRA is funded with after-tax dollars, so your contributions can usually come out tax- and penalty-free at any age. The complexity starts with earnings: pull those before age 59 and a half, and before the account is five years old, and you are generally looking at ordinary income tax plus a 10% early-withdrawal penalty.

## What the calculator actually estimates

A good Roth IRA early withdrawal calculator separates your basis (contributions) from growth (earnings), applies the IRS ordering rules, and only taxes the portion treated as earnings. Feed it three numbers, total contributions, current balance, and the amount you want to withdraw, and it returns the taxable share, the penalty, and your net proceeds.

## The ordering rules in plain English

The IRS removes money in a fixed order: contributions first, then converted amounts, then earnings. Because contributions come out first and are always tax-free, small withdrawals often carry no tax at all. The penalty only bites once you reach the earnings layer.

## Exceptions that waive the 10% penalty

A first-time home purchase (up to $10,000), qualified education costs, certain medical expenses, and a permanent disability can each waive the penalty on earnings. The tax may still apply, so model both numbers before you decide.

## Run the numbers before you withdraw

Before tapping the account, estimate the cost, weigh it against other options, and remember that withdrawn growth loses its tax-free future. The calculator turns an abstract rule into a single net figure you can act on.
